Most journaling advice collapses into "just write." That's true and useless. The real practice is knowing *which* technique to reach for when — racing thoughts at 11pm needs a different tool than a Sunday review.

Here are seven journaling techniques that hold up under research and real life, with examples and when each one earns its place.

## 1. Interstitial journaling

Coined by Tony Stubblebine, interstitial journaling means writing a few sentences *between* tasks throughout the day. Not a morning ritual — a context switch.

**When to use it:** between meetings, before starting deep work, after a difficult conversation.

**How:** Open your journal, timestamp the entry, write 2–3 sentences. *"Just finished the sync. Felt defensive when Maya pushed on the timeline. Next: write the spec, but first 10 min of nothing."*

It works because it converts transition time — usually lost to scrolling — into a tiny metabolizing window. The pattern across a week becomes its own diagnostic.

## 2. Reflective prompts

A prompt is a question you answer in writing. The good ones are specific enough to bypass your usual scripts.

**Examples that actually work:**

- What did I avoid today, and what was I afraid of?
- What did I assume that I didn't verify?
- If a friend described my week back to me, what would they notice?
- What am I pretending not to know?

**When to use it:** end of day, end of week, or any time your mind feels stuck on a loop that won't name itself.

## 3. Morning pages (the 5-minute version)

Julia Cameron's original is three handwritten pages every morning. The modern version: five minutes, brain-dump style, no editing. Empty the overnight cache before the day fills it back up.

## 4. Cognitive defusion (the "I'm having the thought that…" trick)

From Acceptance and Commitment Therapy. Instead of writing *"I'm a fraud,"* write *"I'm having the thought that I'm a fraud."* Then *"I notice I'm having the thought that I'm a fraud."*

**When to use it:** when a single sentence keeps repeating in your head. The reframe puts a millimeter of distance between you and the thought — enough to look at it instead of through it.

## 5. The unsent letter

Write a full letter to the person you're stuck on — boss, ex, parent, past self — with no intention to send. Say the thing.

**When to use it:** unresolved conversations, grief, resentment that won't compost. The brain treats the writing as partial completion, which is often enough to let the loop close.

## 6. The Sunday review

A weekly pass over the last seven days. Three questions:

1. What worked? (be specific — a moment, not a category)
2. What drained me? (same — a moment)
3. What's one small change for next week?

**When to use it:** weekly. The compounding pattern is the point — most people only see their life in real time and never review the tape.

## 7. AI-reflected journaling

The newest technique on the list. You write the entry; an AI reads it back to you and names the pattern underneath — not advice, a read.

**When to use it:** when the entry feels tangled and you can't see what you're circling. A model trained on reflective dialogue (this is what NexoMind does) can compress 400 words of overthinking into the one sentence underneath: *"You're not actually angry at the email — you're angry that you said yes in the first place."*

It's not therapy. It's the friend-who-listens-carefully role, available at 2am.

## Which one should you start with?

If you've never journaled consistently, start with **interstitial journaling** — the bar is lowest and the feedback is fastest.

If you've journaled before but it stopped working, try **reflective prompts** — most people quit because they ran out of things to say, not because the practice failed them.

If you're using journaling to manage anxiety or overthinking, pair **morning pages** with **AI-reflected journaling**. The first empties the buffer; the second names what was in it.

The technique matters less than the consistency. Pick one. Do it for two weeks before you change it.
