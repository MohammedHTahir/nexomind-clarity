The day goes quiet. The lights are off. And then your mind, which had been busy all day, finally has space — and uses it to replay the conversation you had at 2pm, the email you forgot to send, the thing you should have said three years ago.

This isn't a flaw. It's what an unprocessed day sounds like.

## Why overthinking gets louder at night

During the day, your attention is occupied. Tasks, screens, conversations, decisions — they all consume the bandwidth that, at night, suddenly belongs to your thoughts. The thoughts haven't changed. The room they're in just got bigger.

Add tiredness to the mix and your brain's filter — the part that decides what's worth thinking about — gets weaker. So things you'd normally dismiss in daylight feel urgent at 1am.

## A 4-step practice to quiet the loop

You don't need to stop the thoughts. You need to give them somewhere to go.

1. **Name what's looping.** Out loud or on paper: "I keep thinking about the meeting." Naming it makes it finite.
2. **Ask one question.** Not "why am I like this?" — that opens the loop. Ask: "What is this thought trying to protect me from?"
3. **Write the answer in one sentence.** No editing. The first answer is usually the truest.
4. **Close the loop.** Tell yourself: "I've heard it. I'll come back to it tomorrow." Mean it.

## Why writing works

Thoughts in your head are loops. Thoughts on paper are lines. A loop has no end; a line does. The act of writing forces the thought to commit to a shape — and once it has a shape, your brain stops checking on it.
