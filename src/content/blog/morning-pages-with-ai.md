Julia Cameron's "morning pages" — three pages of longhand, every morning, no editing — is one of the most quietly powerful creative practices ever written down. It works because it forces your brain to empty its overnight cache before the day fills it back up.

The problem: three pages takes 25–40 minutes. Most people don't have that. So they don't do it.

Here's a modernized version that captures the spirit of the practice in five minutes.

## The 5-minute routine

**Minute 1 — Brain dump.** Open your journal. Write whatever's on top, in any order. Don't structure it. "Tired. Annoyed at the email. Why did I say yes to Saturday." Keep going until the buffer's empty.

**Minute 2 — One feeling.** Pick the strongest one. Name it in one word. *Anxious. Heavy. Restless. Excited.* Don't justify it.

**Minute 3 — One question.** Write the question your feeling is asking. "Am I overcommitted?" "Did I disappoint them?" "What am I avoiding?" The first question is almost always the right one.

**Minute 4 — Let AI reflect it back.** This is the modern part. Hand the entry to a private AI reflection tool (NexoMind, for instance) and let it surface the pattern underneath. Not advice. A read.

**Minute 5 — One sentence forward.** "Today I'll send the email." "Today I'll let the Saturday thing be uncertain." One concrete sentence about the next 24 hours.

## Why this works

The original morning pages worked through volume — three pages of writing exhausts the surface noise so the real signal can surface. AI compresses that. You don't need three pages if a model can find the signal in 200 words.

The discipline isn't the page count. It's showing up daily, before the day takes over. Five minutes you'll do is infinitely better than thirty you won't.

## A note on resistance

Some mornings the page will feel fake. Write anyway. The practice is the point, not the output. Within two weeks you'll notice you're starting the day from a different floor.
